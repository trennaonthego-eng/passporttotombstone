## Market Signal Brief

*Source agent: market-signal-researcher. Basis: business-brief.md plus general knowledge only. No web research was done, so no search volumes, view counts or competitor data are cited. Everything beyond what the brief states is labeled (Inference).*

### Demand Pattern
- The brief names the audience as coaches, consultants, creators, service providers and entrepreneurs. They "know AI is important" but are "overwhelmed by tools" and unsure what to build first. The core problem is sequencing, not awareness.
- (Inference) The likely searches are outcome-led and system-led: "AI for coaches," "AI business system," "Claude Code for business," "AI offer/lead magnet generator," "AI workflow that makes money." A bare tool name like "Claude Code tutorial" is less likely to convert, and the brief explicitly rules out generic tool tutorials.
- (Inference) "Claude Code" is typically associated with developers. A non-technical entrepreneur audience using it for positioning, offers and funnels is a differentiated angle. It is also a possible credibility gap, because viewers may assume it is not for them.
- (Inference) The "one idea in, five assets out" output (positioning, offer, YouTube angles, lead magnet, follow-up sequence) maps to the standard coach/consultant launch checklist. Viewers already know they need those assets, so the video is easy to understand quickly.

### Audience Psychology
- Wants: a single starting point and a visible, finished business asset stack, not another tool list.
- Fears: wasting time on the wrong tool, falling behind competitors, and producing generic AI slop that sounds like everyone else.
- Misunderstands (Inference): that better prompts or the newest tool are the lever. The brief's thesis ("the money is in systems, not tools") directly reframes this.
- Secretly hopes (Inference): that one workflow can replace a strategist, copywriter and marketer, and that they do not need to be technical.
- Sophistication (Inference): mixed, skewing intermediate-skeptical. They have tried ChatGPT-style prompting, are tired of hype, and want proof of output quality.

### Risk
- It feels generic if the sample business idea is vague (such as "a coaching business") and the five outputs read like default ChatGPT copy.
- It feels unbelievable if the outputs are shown without any edits, the process behind them, or a real or plausible business result. Revenue claims need real proof, so do not imply income the creator cannot show.
- It feels like a dev tutorial if file structure and config take more than about 2 minutes.
- It undermines its own thesis if the video lists "agents" as tools instead of showing how they chain into a system.
- (Inference) The "beginner-friendly but advanced" constraint can split the audience: beginners get lost on agents, and advanced viewers find it shallow.

### Strategic Recommendation
- Title and thumbnail around the outcome, not the tool: e.g., "One idea in, a full launch system out," with Claude Code as a secondary phrase.
- Use one specific, concrete niche idea and show each agent's output feeding the next (positioning, then offer, then angles, then lead magnet, then follow-up), so the "systems, not tools" point is visible.
- Keep setup under 2 minutes, then spend most of the runtime on output review. Show one human edit to prove quality control.
- Open with the cost of the pain (months of undecided positioning or no lead magnet), then deliver a payoff in the first 60 seconds.
- Make the Revenue Agent template the natural next step, and offer the workshop for people who want help adapting it. Do not claim specific revenue results unless they are real and shown.
